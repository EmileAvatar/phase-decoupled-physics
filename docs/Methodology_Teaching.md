# Methodology (Teaching) — A Leveled Curriculum for Teaching Research Methodology

**Type:** Teaching/curriculum companion (not physics content — see Section 0).
**Companion to:** `Methodology.md` (PDTP's own condensed, practical checklist),
`Methodology_Math.md`, `Methodology_Science.md`, and `Methodology_Human_AI.md`
(the failure-modes document). This is the fifth and last of that family, and the
odd one out: the other four are written for people already doing the research.
This one is written for a teacher who wants to teach the *process* those four
documents describe — to students who may start with no science or math
background at all, all the way up to university research-methods level.
**Purpose:** turn several years of a real, warts-and-all research project into
reusable classroom material — worked examples, leveled explanations, can-do
checklists a teacher can use to place and track a student, sample lesson plans,
and a one-page tracking grid. Written so a teacher does not need a physics
background to use the early tiers, and knows exactly what background is needed
before attempting the later ones.

## 0. Read This First — What This Document Is and Is Not

**This document teaches a PROCESS, not a set of physics claims.** Every worked
example below is drawn from PDTP (Phase-Decoupled Transport Physics), a
speculative, not-experimentally-validated framework — but in every case, what
is being taught is *how the checking was done*, never *that the physics is
true*. A student should come away from this material able to explain why a
claim needs to survive ten independent checks rather than one, not able to
recite "gravity is emergent phase-locking" as settled fact, because it is not
settled, and may never be.

**This is deliberate, not a hedge.** PDTP was chosen as the source material
specifically *because* it documents its own reasoning in unusual detail — every
equation tagged by how confident it is, every failed attempt written down next
to the successful ones, every internal contradiction that was ever found
recorded along with how it was resolved. That transparency is what makes it
useful teaching material. The physics claims themselves might turn out to be
wrong. **If they do, the methodology in this document — and in the four it
draws from — should still be worth teaching on its own terms.** That is the
actual reason this document exists separately from the physics: so the
process survives even if the specific theory does not.

**What to tell a student who asks "so is this real?"** Something honest and
short: *"Nobody knows yet — it hasn't been tested against experiment. What we
ARE going to learn from it is how careful people check whether an idea like
this holds together before they trust it. That skill is useful whether this
particular idea turns out to be right or wrong."* Section 10 (FAQ) has more
scripted answers for common student questions.

**What this document is not:**
- Not a physics curriculum. It does not teach general relativity, quantum
  mechanics, or Lagrangian mechanics as content to be learned for their own
  sake (a teacher wanting that should look elsewhere; the physics vocabulary
  that appears here is only ever in service of a methodology example).
- Not a claim that PDTP is validated, mainstream, or peer-reviewed. It is
  none of those things, and every tier below says so explicitly.
- Not a replacement for `Methodology.md`, `Methodology_Math.md`,
  `Methodology_Science.md`, or `Methodology_Human_AI.md` — it is a simplified,
  leveled front door to them. Tier 4 hands the reader off to all four directly.

## 1. How to Use This Document

**The tier system.** Five tiers (0-4), each covering the *same* five core
skills (Section 2) at increasing depth. A student does not "finish" Skill B
at Tier 1 and never see it again — they meet it five times, each time with
more precision and less hand-holding. This mirrors how the real methodology
documents work: `Methodology.md`'s Sudoku check is the same idea a Tier 0
student meets as "check it against many clues, not one."

**Placing a student.** Ask three questions, no preparation required:
1. "If I told you a rule, and later broke my own rule without saying why,
   would you notice?" (Tests Skill A intuitively.)
2. "If someone claims something and says 'no matter what happens, I'll still
   be right' — is that a strong claim or a weak one?" (Tests Skill C intuitively.)
3. Ask what "hypothesis," "prediction," and "falsifiable" mean, if anything.

A student who engages with (1) and (2) but has no vocabulary for (3) starts
at **Tier 0**. A student who has the vocabulary but has never formally used it
starts at **Tier 1 or 2**. A student who can already state Popper's
falsifiability criterion unprompted is ready to skip to **Tier 3**. There is
no test to place a student directly into Tier 4 — it assumes Tier 3's formal
vocabulary is already comfortable.

**Moving a student up a tier.** Use that tier's can-do checklist (present at
the end of every tier section) as the gate. All five boxes checked, in the
student's own words (not recited) — move up. This is deliberately not a
written exam; the can-do statements are things a student should be able to
*do* (explain, spot, design), not recall.

**The can-do checklist format.** Every tier ends with five statements, one per
core skill, phrased as "I can..." — written for the teacher to check off after
watching or listening to a student, not for the student to self-grade.

## 2. The Five Core Skills

Everything in this document is five ideas, taught five times at increasing
depth. Naming them once here so the pattern is visible before the tiers bury
them in examples.

| Skill | One line | What it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Does it hang together?** | Internal consistency — do the parts of a claim agree with each other? | Accepting a claim that quietly contradicts itself |
| **B. Check it against many things, not one** | Overdetermination — the "Sudoku" idea: one match could be luck, ten matches is evidence | Cherry-picking the one fact that happens to agree |
| **C. Could this be shown wrong?** | Falsifiability — a real claim says what result would disprove it | Unfalsifiable claims dressed up as testable ones |
| **D. "I assumed it" vs. "I proved it" vs. "I'm guessing"** | Separating confidence levels — assumption, derivation, verification, speculation are different things | Conflating a guess with a proof |
| **E. A "no" is still an answer** | Honest negative results and self-correction | Hiding, spinning, or being ashamed of a failed attempt |

**Where these come from, for a teacher who wants the full-strength version:**
Skill A and E map most directly onto `Methodology.md` Section 3 (consistency
checks) and Section 5 (handling negative results). Skill B is `Methodology.md`'s
own signature tool, the Sudoku Consistency Check. Skill C is Popper's
falsifiability criterion (`Methodology_Science.md` Section 2). Skill D is
PDTP's own equation-tagging convention (`[ASSUMED]` / `[DERIVED]` /
`[VERIFIED]` / `[SPECULATIVE]`, see `CLAUDE.md` and every research doc in
`docs/research/`). None of this is invented for teaching purposes — it is
the literal, everyday working practice of the project, simplified for
presentation, not diluted in substance.

## 3. Tier 0 — Everyday Reasoning

**No science or math vocabulary required.** Everything below can be taught
with no equations at all, using situations any student has already lived
through: games, recipes, shopping, stories.

**A. Does it hang together?** If a friend teaches you a rule for a game, and
twenty minutes later breaks their own rule without explaining why, something
is wrong — either the rule was never really the rule, or there are two
different rules that disagree. *Real example, translated to plain language:*
early in this project, one of its core mathematical rules was written down
one way in one place and the *opposite* way in another place — like a recipe
that says "add sugar" in the ingredient list and "do not add sugar" in the
instructions. Nobody noticed for a while, because each part looked fine on
its own. It was only caught by checking the two parts against each other.
Once caught, testing showed only one version actually produced something
stable — a bit like discovering that only the "add sugar" version actually
makes a cake that rises, so that's the version that got kept.

**B. Check it against many things, not one.** If someone hands you a number
and says "this solves the puzzle," and it fits one clue, that is weak
evidence — plenty of wrong numbers fit one clue by luck. If it fits *ten*
different clues at once, that is strong evidence, because it is very hard for
a wrong answer to accidentally satisfy that many independent checks. This
project has a rule: no new idea is trusted until it has been checked against
at least ten already-known facts, not just one.

**C. Could this be shown wrong?** A claim is only useful if you can say, in
advance, what would prove it wrong. "It will rain tomorrow" can be checked.
"Whatever the weather does, I'll say I predicted it" cannot be checked — it
is not really a prediction, no matter how confident it sounds.

**D. "I assumed it" vs. "I proved it" vs. "I'm guessing."** There is a real
difference between "I think this is true" (a guess), "this follows step by
step from something we already trust" (worked out), and "we tested this and
it held up" (confirmed). Careful people always say which one they mean. This
project labels literally every single equation with one of four tags — plain
labels meaning "this is a starting assumption," "this was worked out
step-by-step," "this was checked and confirmed," or "this is speculation" —
specifically so nobody, not even the people who wrote it, forgets which is
which six months later.

**E. A "no" is still an answer.** If you carefully test an idea and it turns
out to be wrong, that is not a wasted afternoon — you have learned something
true (this particular idea does not work), and often *exactly where* it stops
working, which is useful information for whatever you try next. Hiding a "no"
answer, or pretending it was a "yes," is the actual failure — getting a clear
"no" is not. This project keeps its "no, that didn't work" results written
down in full, right next to the "yes" ones, for exactly this reason.

**Tier 0 can-do checklist:**
- [ ] Can spot when two parts of a rule, story, or plan contradict each other
- [ ] Can explain, in their own words, why checking something against many
      facts is stronger evidence than checking it against just one
- [ ] Can give one example of a claim that could be proven wrong, and one
      that could not
- [ ] Can tell apart "I think," "I worked this out," and "I checked this"
- [ ] Can explain why "that didn't work" is still a useful thing to learn

**Suggested activity:** Give pairs of students two short index-card "rules for
a made-up game," where one card secretly contradicts itself between its first
and second half. Have them find the contradiction without being told to look
for one, then explain in their own words why it matters. No physics, no math.

## 4. Tier 1 — Foundational Science Literacy

**New vocabulary introduced:** hypothesis (an educated guess specific enough
to test), evidence, prediction. Builds directly on Tier 0 — same five skills,
now with names attached.

**Worked example for this tier — a guess that failed ten times, on purpose:**
Researchers once proposed a specific value for a constant in this project's
equations (called K), based on a reasonable-sounding guess about its origin.
That is a **hypothesis** — not a wild guess, but not yet trusted either. They
worked out what that hypothesis **predicted** for ten different already-known
physics facts, then checked: did the number match reality in all ten cases?
**The result: zero out of ten matched.** Not "close" — off by a huge amount,
consistently. That could have been the end of it. Instead, they noticed
*something specific*: the amount they were off by was the *same ratio* every
single time, and that ratio turned out to exactly equal a comparison between
two very different mass scales in physics. The "failure" pointed straight at
one of the most famous unsolved puzzles in real physics (why some scales in
nature are so much bigger than others than others, called the *hierarchy
problem*) — not a calculation mistake, but a genuine, useful discovery hiding
inside a wrong guess.

**Why this is the right story for this tier:** it shows a hypothesis being
tested against *evidence* (Skill B, ten checks) with a *falsifiable*
outcome (Skill C — the guess really could have been shown wrong, and it was),
and it shows Skill E directly: the "no" was not thrown away, it was studied,
and it turned out to be more interesting than a "yes" would have been.

**A. Does it hang together?** Now with vocabulary: before testing a
hypothesis against evidence, first check the hypothesis is even internally
consistent — does it contradict some other part of the same theory? Only a
self-consistent hypothesis is worth testing further.

**B. Check it against many things, not one.** This is what "ten out of ten"
in the worked example means: ten separate, independent predictions, checked
one at a time, scored honestly.

**C. Could this be shown wrong?** The K-constant hypothesis passed this test
easily — it made specific numerical predictions that could (and did) fail.
Contrast with a hypothesis like "K has some value, we're not sure what" —
that version predicts nothing and could never fail, which is a sign it is not
yet a real, testable hypothesis at all.

**D. "I assumed it" vs. "I proved it" vs. "I'm guessing."** The K-constant
value started as an assumption (a specific number chosen for a stated reason,
not yet checked) — never presented as proven until (or unless) the evidence
supported it. It did not, so it stayed labeled as a rejected assumption, not
quietly reclassified as something stronger.

**E. A "no" is still an answer.** The full worked example above. A zero-out-
of-ten result was not hidden — it was written up in as much detail as any
success, because the *pattern* of the failure was itself the finding.

**Tier 1 can-do checklist:**
- [ ] Can define hypothesis, evidence, and prediction in their own words
- [ ] Can explain why a hypothesis must make a specific prediction, not a
      vague one, to be worth testing
- [ ] Can describe the K-constant story and say what made the "failure"
      valuable rather than wasted
- [ ] Can identify, given a real hypothesis, whether it has been assumed,
      worked out, or checked against evidence
- [ ] Can propose one way they'd test a simple hypothesis of their own

**Suggested activity:** Have students write a specific, testable hypothesis
about something observable in the classroom (not physics-specific — plant
growth, reaction times, anything countable), predict three separate,
independent outcomes it implies, and design a way to check all three before
running anything.

## 5. Tier 2 — Secondary / Pre-University

**New vocabulary introduced:** falsifiability, named after Karl Popper; a
real, simplified consistency scorecard (the "Sudoku Consistency Check" by
name); the idea that two things can share a name but not be the same thing.

**Falsifiability, formally:** a scientific claim must specify, in advance,
an observation that would count against it. Popper's own example: "all swans
are white" is falsifiable (one black swan disproves it); "there exists a
swan-god who sometimes makes swans black for reasons we cannot know" is not
falsifiable — it can absorb any observation, which is precisely why it
explains nothing. (Full treatment: `Methodology_Science.md` Section 2.)

**A real (simplified) Sudoku Consistency Check scorecard:**

| # | Known fact checked against | Does the new idea agree? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Earlier, independently-confirmed result A | Match |
| 2 | Earlier, independently-confirmed result B | Match |
| 3 | A basic conservation law (nothing appears from nowhere) | Match |
| 4 | The result in the limit where the new effect should vanish | Match |
| 5 | A completely different derivation of a related quantity | Match |
| 6 | ... (and so on, ten or more rows) | |

Score reported honestly as "N matches, M contradictions out of T tests" —
never rounded up, never a single passing test presented as if it were the
whole picture.

**Worked example for this tier — two things with the same name, checked, and
found to be different:** In this project's own recent work, two different
formulas both used the same short symbol, "g," for what looked like the same
underlying quantity — one used in a formula about the shape of the whole
universe's expansion, the other used in a completely different, smaller-scale
formula. Because they shared a name, it would have been easy to assume they
were interchangeable. A careful check — literally just tracking the *units*
each one carries through the math step by step (Skill A: does it hang
together, applied rigorously) — showed the two "g"s differed by **122 powers
of ten**. Not close. If someone had swapped one formula's "g" into the other
without checking, the answer would have been wrong by more than a hundred
orders of magnitude, silently. The check did not just avoid an error — it
revealed that two things everyone had been casually calling by the same name
were, in fact, two different physical objects that happen to share a symbol.

**A. Does it hang together?** The worked example above IS this skill, applied
formally: tracking units through an equation step by step to check nothing
sneaks in or drops out unnoticed.

**B. Check it against many things, not one.** The scorecard table above —
this is literally what it looks like in practice, not just an idea.

**C. Could this be shown wrong?** Popper's criterion, stated formally. A good
classroom test: give students five real or invented claims and have them sort
each into "falsifiable" or "not falsifiable," defending each choice.

**D. "I assumed it" vs. "I proved it" vs. "I'm guessing."** Introduce the
actual four-way labels this project uses on every equation: `[ASSUMED]` (a
starting point, not yet justified further), `[DERIVED]` (follows step by step
from something already trusted), `[VERIFIED]` (checked, e.g. by an
independent symbolic-algebra tool, not just eyeballed), `[SPECULATIVE]` (an
idea being explored, explicitly not yet either derived or verified).

**E. A "no" is still an answer.** Extend Tier 1: a "no" that comes with a
*pattern* (like the ten-out-of-ten failure, or the 122-powers-of-ten mismatch
above) is more valuable than a "no" with no pattern at all — it tells you
something specific to go check next, not just that you were wrong.

**Tier 2 can-do checklist:**
- [ ] Can state Popper's falsifiability criterion and apply it to sort five
      claims into falsifiable / not falsifiable
- [ ] Can read a Sudoku-style scorecard and correctly report the
      match/contradiction count
- [ ] Can explain, using the "two things named g" example, why checking units
      or definitions carefully can reveal a hidden error even when a formula
      "looks" right
- [ ] Can correctly label a set of sample claims as assumed, derived,
      verified, or speculative
- [ ] Can describe what makes a negative result more useful than another —
      the presence of a specific, checkable pattern

**Suggested activity:** Give students a short set of physics-adjacent or
everyday claims sharing a common term (e.g., two different uses of "weight,"
or "power," or "energy" in casual speech vs. a technical sense) and have them
determine, by tracing definitions, whether the two uses are really the same
thing or different things wearing the same word.

## 6. Tier 3 — University Introductory / Research Methods

**New material introduced:** Pólya's four-phase problem-solving framework;
Kuhn's paradigms (normal vs. revolutionary science); the full
hypothesis-prediction-test-revise cycle; the requirement that a *new* idea be
checked not just against fresh evidence but against *everything the old
theory already explained*.

**Pólya's four phases** (`Methodology_Math.md` Section 1, citing Pólya 1945,
*How to Solve It*): (1) understand the problem — what is actually being
asked; (2) devise a plan — has a related problem been solved before; (3)
carry out the plan, checking each step; (4) look back — does the answer make
sense, and could the method be reused elsewhere. Worth teaching explicitly:
most of the "obvious in hindsight" moves in this project's history are
Pólya's Phase 2 in action — recognizing a new problem as structurally similar
to one already solved.

**Kuhn's paradigms** (`Methodology_Science.md` Section 3, citing Kuhn 1962,
*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*): most science, most of the time,
is "normal science" — solving puzzles within an accepted framework, not
questioning its foundations. A "paradigm shift" — replacing the framework
itself — is rare, and is not simply "someone had a better idea"; it typically
requires the old paradigm to have accumulated unresolved anomalies first.
Useful for a student to understand: a speculative project like this one is
explicitly *not* claiming to have completed a paradigm shift — it is
proposing a candidate framework and testing whether it is even internally
viable, which is a much earlier and more modest stage.

**Worked example for this tier — testing a new idea against everything the
old idea already got right:** This project has a rule, stated formally: any
time an existing theory is extended or modified, the new version must be
checked against *every* result the old version already established, not just
whatever new predictions motivated the change. When one such extension was
introduced, the team went back and re-derived **sixteen** previously-
established results — Newton's laws, the recovery of general-relativity-like
behavior, and others — one at a time, inside the new, extended framework, to
check that nothing that used to work had silently broken. All sixteen
passed. This is the formal version of Skill B (check it against many things)
applied to an entire *theory*, not just a single number: the bar for
replacing or extending an accepted framework is not "does the new idea sound
good," it is "does the new idea still explain everything the old idea
explained, plus something more."

**A. Does it hang together?** At this tier: internal consistency is checked
not just within one calculation but across an entire theoretical framework —
does extending the theory in one place quietly break something proven
somewhere else in it?

**B. Check it against many things, not one.** The sixteen-result
re-derivation above is this skill at full scale: not ten arbitrary facts, but
literally everything the prior framework was known to get right.

**C. Could this be shown wrong?** Introduce the idea of a *kill test* — a
specific, named observation that would rule a hypothesis out entirely, stated
in advance, before the relevant data exists. A strong research program names
its own kill tests rather than waiting to be challenged.

**D. "I assumed it" vs. "I proved it" vs. "I'm guessing."** Introduce the
full hypothesis → prediction → test → revise cycle as a formal loop, and the
idea that a result can be internally consistent and pass every check *and
still be circular* (its truth was assumed somewhere upstream) — consistency
alone does not prove correctness, it only fails to disprove it.

**E. A "no" is still an answer.** Introduce the idea of *declaring exhaustion
explicitly*: when several reasonable approaches to a hard problem have all
failed, saying so plainly ("we tried the standard paths, none worked, here is
where a fundamentally different strategy is needed") is itself a valid,
citable research contribution — not an admission of failure to be minimized.

**Tier 3 can-do checklist:**
- [ ] Can walk through Pólya's four phases using a problem of their own choosing
- [ ] Can explain the difference between normal and revolutionary science,
      and correctly classify a few example scenarios
- [ ] Can explain why extending a theory requires re-checking old results,
      not just testing new predictions, using the sixteen-result example
- [ ] Can state a "kill test" in advance for a hypothesis of their own
- [ ] Can explain why "internally consistent" is necessary but not
      sufficient for "correct," and give an example of a self-consistent but
      circular argument

**Suggested activity/assignment:** Have students choose a claim from a field
outside physics (economics, biology, even a sports statistic), and design
their own ten-point Sudoku-style consistency check for it — ten independent,
already-known facts it should be consistent with — then actually run the
check and report an honest score, contradictions included.

## 7. Tier 4 — Advanced / Graduate Research Practice

**New material introduced:** the full eight-step Sudoku Consistency Check
procedure; Lakatos's "proofs and refutations" (a negative result *refines* a
claim rather than simply killing it); a formal anti-hand-waving discipline for
checking that a piece of analysis actually computed what it claims to have
computed; an escalation procedure for when normal methods have stalled on a
genuinely hard problem. This tier assumes Tier 3's vocabulary is already
comfortable, and functions as a bridge directly into `Methodology.md`,
`Methodology_Math.md`, `Methodology_Science.md`, and `Methodology_Human_AI.md`
themselves — a student finishing this tier should be able to read all four
without translation.

**The full Sudoku Consistency Check, as an eight-step procedure**
(`Methodology.md`, full statement): (1) assume the new value or equation is
correct; (2) substitute it into ten or more independently-established
equations; (3) check compatibility with the broader accepted framework, not
just isolated facts; (4) check it holds under every known *extension* of the
framework, not only the original version; (5) verify it does not silently
assume the very thing it is meant to establish (a circularity check); (6)
score each result as a ratio — close to 1.00 is a match, anything else is a
contradiction; (7) *explain* every contradiction rather than discarding it;
(8) report the full scorecard honestly, as "N matches, M contradictions out
of T tests" — including the failures.

**Lakatos: a refutation refines, it does not just kill** (`Methodology_Math.md`
Section 4, citing Lakatos 1976, *Proofs and Refutations*): when a
counterexample is found against a general claim, the mature response is
usually not "the claim was worthless," it is "the claim needs a precise
condition added to exclude this case" — the counterexample sharpens the
theorem rather than discarding it. Contrast with an immature response:
quietly ignoring the counterexample, or abandoning an otherwise-sound idea
over one exception that could have been fenced off precisely.

**The RECHECK discipline — does the code actually compute what it claims?**
(`CLAUDE.md`, "RECHECK functions" section): a five-question checklist applied
to *any* piece of quantitative analysis, not just PDTP's own: (1) does every
number in the output come from an arithmetic expression using real inputs, or
is the "expected" answer typed directly into the result? (2) does printed
narrative text ("the ratio is 2π") match a variable that was actually
computed, or is it a hardcoded string? (3) does the final consistency check
read the *computed* value, or does it silently recompute its own version
in a way that would hide a bug in the first calculation? (4) are boolean
"pass/fail" flags themselves computed from a real comparison, or hardcoded to
`True`? (5) can every claimed result be traced, link by link, from raw inputs
through to the final answer with no hardcoded shortcut anywhere in the chain?
A single hardcoded step anywhere breaks the whole chain's credibility, even
if every other step is honest.

**Worked example for this tier — the methodology catching a real mistake, in
real time:** A four-month-old document in this project had never been checked
with independent symbolic algebra — it was explicitly labeled, in its own
header, as "not yet verified." When it finally was checked, the check did not
simply confirm what was already written; it *disagreed* with three of the
four core equations. Before trusting that disagreement, the check was
validated against the *fourth* equation first — which the checking tool
reproduced exactly, matching the original document. Only once the tool had
proven itself reliable on a known-good case was its disagreement on the other
three trusted: they had a genuine sign error, several months old, never
caught until an independent method was finally applied. A second, unrelated
error was found the same session, in a different section of the same
document: a claimed pair of numbers was checked with an elementary rule any
matrix has to obey (its eigenvalues must add up to a fixed, independently-
computable total). The claimed numbers failed that check by **ten orders of
magnitude** — not a rounding issue, a genuine, previously unnoticed
arithmetic error. Both mistakes were corrected in place, documented alongside
the process that caught them, and the document's confidence label was
downgraded accordingly rather than quietly patched over.

**Why this is the right story for this tier:** every one of the five core
skills appears at once, at full strength — Skill A (the internal
disagreement between the document's own sections), Skill B (checking against
a known mathematical fact — the eigenvalue-sum rule — not just "does it look
right"), Skill C (the check was falsifiable and it actually failed, twice),
Skill D (the document's confidence label was formally downgraded, not left
overstated), and Skill E (both errors were written up in full, not minimized,
right in the same document that once claimed the numbers were fine).

**A. Does it hang together?** At this tier: validate your checking *method*
against a known-good case before trusting its verdict on a disputed case —
this is what separates a real disagreement from a tool malfunction.

**B. Check it against many things, not one.** Basic mathematical identities
(an eigenvalue sum equaling a trace, a conservation law, a symmetry) make
excellent, cheap, high-confidence checks — they require no new theory, only
correct arithmetic, which makes them very hard to fool.

**C. Could this be shown wrong?** At this tier, falsifiability is applied
recursively — not just to the theory's claims, but to the *verification
process itself*. A check that can never fail is not really checking anything.

**D. "I assumed it" vs. "I proved it" vs. "I'm guessing."** The formal
downgrade of a document's status label (from "not yet verified" to
"partially verified, errors found and corrected") IS this skill, enacted as
an actual editorial decision with a paper trail, not just a private
realization.

**E. A "no" is still an answer.** The full worked example above, at maximum
strength: two real mistakes, caught, explained, fixed, and left visible in
the historical record rather than erased.

**When normal methods stall — the escalation procedure:** for a genuinely
hard, foundational problem, after several independent reasonable approaches
have all failed, this project's practice is to escalate explicitly rather
than keep circling: go through *every single item* on a standing problem-
solving checklist (`Methodology.md`'s Problem-Solving Checklist, Sections
1-8), answer "have we tried this, what happened" for each one — including
items that seem irrelevant — document all the answers in writing, and only
then form a new plan based on whatever was never actually tried. This is
slow by design; it exists precisely so that a hard problem is never abandoned
just because the first few obvious approaches did not work.

**Tier 4 can-do checklist:**
- [ ] Can list and apply all eight steps of a full Sudoku Consistency Check
- [ ] Can explain Lakatos's view of what a counterexample should do to a
      claim, and contrast it with simply discarding the claim
- [ ] Can apply the five-question RECHECK discipline to a piece of sample
      code or analysis and correctly identify any hardcoded "results"
- [ ] Can explain why validating a checking method against a known-good case
      matters before trusting its verdict on a disputed case, using the
      worked example
- [ ] Can describe the escalation procedure for a stalled hard problem, and
      explain why it is deliberately exhaustive rather than efficient

**Suggested activity/assignment:** Give students a short piece of analysis
code (or a worked derivation) with one deliberately hardcoded "result" hidden
among otherwise-real calculations, and have them find it using only the
RECHECK questions — no answer key, just the checklist.

**Where to go from here:** a student comfortable with this entire tier is
ready to read `Methodology.md`, `Methodology_Math.md`, `Methodology_Science.md`,
and `Methodology_Human_AI.md` directly, in full, with no further translation
needed — and, if they want to see the methodology applied to live,
in-progress work rather than only finished examples, the project's own
`TODO_04.md` / `TODO_05.md` files and `docs/research/` folder are a complete,
public record of it happening in real time, successes and failures alike.

## 8. Teacher's Tracking Grid

One page, five skills by five tiers, for marking a specific student's actual
progress. Each cell is that tier's version of the skill, condensed to a
phrase — use it as a quick-reference summary, not a replacement for the full
can-do checklists in each tier section above.

| Skill | Tier 0 | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 3 | Tier 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Hangs together?** | Spot a contradiction in a rule/story | Check a hypothesis is self-consistent first | Trace units/definitions step by step | Check consistency across a whole framework | Validate the checking method itself first |
| **B. Many checks, not one** | Explain why 10 clues beat 1 | Read a 10-check pass/fail count | Read/build a Sudoku scorecard | Re-derive ALL prior results, not just new ones | Use basic math identities as cheap, strong checks |
| **C. Falsifiable?** | Sort a falsifiable vs. not-falsifiable claim | Explain why a hypothesis needs a specific prediction | State Popper's criterion; sort real claims | State a "kill test" in advance | Apply falsifiability to the check itself |
| **D. Assumed/proved/guess** | Tell apart "think" / "worked out" / "checked" | Label a real hypothesis correctly | Apply the 4-way status labels | Explain consistent-but-circular | Formally re-label a result, with a paper trail |
| **E. "No" is an answer** | Explain why a failed test is useful | Describe a real 0/10 result and its value | Explain why a *patterned* "no" beats a random one | Declare exhaustion explicitly as a contribution | Document real errors found+fixed, left visible |

**Using the grid with a class:** a simple version is a checkbox per cell per
student, filled in as they demonstrate each skill (via the tier's suggested
activity, a lesson assessment, or informal observation). A student does not
need to complete a tier row left-to-right before starting the next tier down
a column — skills develop unevenly, and the grid is meant to show *where*
a student needs more work, not to gate progress row by row.

## 9. Sample Lesson Plans

One brief plan per tier. Each assumes a single class period and can be
stretched across two if the discussion runs long — that is normal for this
material and not a sign of a plan running behind.

**Tier 0 (no prerequisite).**
*Objective:* students can spot an internal contradiction and explain why
checking a claim multiple ways beats checking it once.
*Warm-up (5 min):* tell two short, contradictory "facts" about yourself and
ask the class to catch it.
*Worked example (10 min):* the "recipe that contradicts itself" story
(Section 3).
*Activity (20 min):* the paired index-card game (Section 3's suggested
activity).
*Discussion (10 min):* "why didn't anyone notice right away? What would have
caught it sooner?"
*Assessment:* Tier 0 can-do checklist, observed during the activity.

**Tier 1 (assumes Tier 0).**
*Objective:* students can define hypothesis/evidence/prediction and explain
why a specific "no" answer can be more valuable than a vague "yes."
*Warm-up (5 min):* ask students to predict, specifically, an outcome of
something observable that day (weather, a class quiz average) — not vaguely.
*Worked example (15 min):* the K-constant "zero out of ten, but a useful
zero" story (Section 4).
*Activity (20 min):* the classroom-hypothesis activity (Section 4's
suggested activity).
*Discussion (10 min):* "was the K-constant guess a failure? Why or why not?"
*Assessment:* Tier 1 can-do checklist.

**Tier 2 (assumes Tier 1).**
*Objective:* students can state Popper's falsifiability criterion, read a
consistency scorecard, and apply the four confidence labels.
*Warm-up (5 min):* five claims on the board, sort falsifiable vs. not, as a
class vote before discussion.
*Worked example (15 min):* the "two things named g" story and the sample
scorecard table (Section 5).
*Activity (20 min):* the shared-word-different-meaning activity (Section 5's
suggested activity).
*Discussion (10 min):* "can you think of another everyday word used two
different ways that could confuse people?"
*Assessment:* Tier 2 can-do checklist.

**Tier 3 (assumes Tier 2; needs a teacher comfortable introducing Pólya and
Kuhn by name, though not deep expertise in either).**
*Objective:* students can apply Pólya's four phases, distinguish normal from
revolutionary science, and explain why extending a theory requires
re-checking old results.
*Warm-up (5 min):* present a puzzle and narrate solving it using Pólya's four
phases out loud.
*Worked example (15 min):* the sixteen-result re-derivation story (Section 6).
*Activity (30-40 min, likely two sessions):* the outside-physics Sudoku-check
assignment (Section 6's suggested activity) — assign as homework, discuss
results the following class.
*Discussion (10 min):* "what would it take for this to become a 'paradigm
shift' rather than normal science? Has it?" (Answer: no — and explaining why
not is itself good practice with Kuhn's framework.)
*Assessment:* Tier 3 can-do checklist plus the graded consistency-check
assignment.

**Tier 4 (assumes Tier 3; the teacher should have read `Methodology.md` and
`CLAUDE.md`'s "RECHECK" section directly before teaching this session).**
*Objective:* students can apply the full eight-step Sudoku check, the RECHECK
discipline, and explain Lakatos's view of counterexamples.
*Warm-up (5 min):* ask "has anyone ever found a real mistake in something
you'd already finished and called done? What did you do next?"
*Worked example (20 min):* the two-real-errors-caught story (Section 7) —
walk through both mistakes and, importantly, *how the check validated itself*
against the one correct equation before trusting its verdict on the others.
*Activity (25 min):* the hidden-hardcoded-result code-review exercise
(Section 7's suggested activity).
*Discussion (10-15 min):* Lakatos vs. simple refutation — "if the code
review had found a mistake, should the whole document have been thrown out?
Why did it get corrected and re-labeled instead?"
*Assessment:* Tier 4 can-do checklist plus the code-review exercise results.

## 10. FAQ for Teachers

**Do I need a physics background to teach this?** For Tiers 0-1, no —
nothing physics-specific is required; the worked examples are translated into
plain language on purpose. For Tier 2, being comfortable with basic algebra
and the idea of "units" (like knowing why you can't add 5 meters to 3
seconds) is enough. Tiers 3-4 benefit from a teacher who has skimmed
`Methodology_Math.md` and `Methodology_Science.md` once beforehand, but do
not require a physics degree — the worked examples are self-contained and
explained in full within this document.

**Is PDTP real? Is it proven?** No. It is explicitly, repeatedly
self-described (including in its own project instructions) as "speculative,"
"not experimentally validated," and "conceptual framework, formalization in
progress." Tell students exactly that. It is a live, ongoing research
project, not a textbook result.

**A student asked whether gravity really is "phase-locking" — what do I
say?** Something like: *"That's the specific hypothesis this project is
testing — nobody knows yet if it's right, including the people working on
it. What we're studying isn't whether that particular idea is true, it's how
you'd carefully check an idea like that — and that skill matters whether this
one turns out to be right or wrong."*

**What if a student wants to go deeper than Tier 4?** Point them to the four
primary documents directly (`Methodology.md`, `Methodology_Math.md`,
`Methodology_Science.md`, `Methodology_Human_AI.md`), and, if they want to see
the process happening live rather than only in finished write-ups, the
project's own `TODO_04.md` / `TODO_05.md` and `docs/research/` folder — a
public, ongoing record including work still in progress and ideas still
unresolved.

**What if PDTP is eventually shown to be wrong?** Then the physics chapter of
this project closes, and — if this document did its job — the methodology
chapter does not. That split is the entire reason this document exists
separately from the physics: so that what is taught here keeps its value on
its own terms, independent of how the underlying theory turns out. See
Section 0.

**Isn't it strange to teach research methodology using an unproven theory as
the example?** It is actually an advantage for this purpose, not a
liability — a *settled*, textbook result rarely shows its own reasoning,
dead ends, and self-corrections in the open; a live, actively-checked,
warts-and-all project does, which is exactly the material a methodology
course needs and a finished textbook usually can't provide.
